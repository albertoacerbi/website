I am interested in analysing large, naturally occurring datasets to investigate human cultural dynamics, especially in modern societies. By “naturally occurring,” I mean datasets that were not created specifically for research purposes (like surveys or census data) but instead represent traces, often digital, of human activity, such as books, songs, movies, or social media interactions.

For example, I have used:

 - [dog breed registration data]({{< ref "publication/2013_plos_a" >}}) to examine whether breed popularity is driven by individual preferences or social influence;
 
 - data from [Google Books]({{< ref "publication/2013_plos_b" >}}), [Project Gutenberg]({{< ref "publication/2017_cog_emo" >}}), and [English language songs]({{< ref "publication/2019_EHS" >}}) to uncover a general decline in the expression of emotions, mostly due to a decrease in _positive_ emotions;
 
 - [baby names records and last.fm playlists]({{< ref "publication/2014_EHB" >}}) to test whether changes in the popularity of cultural traits reveal the learning biases involved;
 
 - [folktale databases]({{< ref "publication/2017_EHB" >}}) to explore whether the complexity of stories correlates with the population size of the societies in which they circulate.

More recently, I have analysed social media data, studying topics such as the spread of [voter fraud conspiracy theories]({{< ref "publication/2023_voter_fraud" >}}) during the 2020 U.S. election, the [diffusion of archaeological content]({{< ref "publication/2025_scirep_archeo" >}}) on Twitter/X, and [the social media response to Netflix's _Our Planet_ documentary]({{< ref "publication/2023_our_planet" >}}). In a [recent preprint](https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/xwktf), I compared many online communities and found that the content features associated with engagement are not universal, but vary considerably from one community to another. I have also used text analysis on other kinds of corpora, for example [speeches by European Commission officials]({{< ref "publication/2026_ICS" >}}), to study how institutions frame disinformation.

This line of work overlaps with what is often called digital humanities, computational humanities, or cultural analytics in sociology. What makes my research cohesive is the use of methods and theories from cultural evolution to generate and test hypotheses with these data.
To put it in a slogan: “Big data need big theory.”
